Policy reference

Google's rules for reviews, and where Always Reviews stands against them

A working reference for anyone writing outreach copy, review replies, or product features for Always Reviews — sourced from Google's own Business Profile help pages, with each rule checked against what the product actually does today.

01
The one hard line

No incentives, ever — for a review, a changed review, or a removed one

Everything else on this page is a best practice. This one is a policy violation, full stop, and the one rule this doc exists to make impossible to miss.

Google's wording

"Offering incentives, like free of cost or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews, change reviews or remove negative reviews is considered fake engagement and is strictly prohibited."

This covers more than it sounds like — not just "leave a review, get 10% off," but also asking a customer to change an existing review, or offering anything to make a negative one disappear. A business found doing this risks profile-level restrictions from Google, independent of anything Always Reviews does.

02
Getting reviews

What Google actually recommends

Three practices, all of which the product already automates or supports — this is the part of Google's guidance Always Reviews exists to execute well.

  • Remind customers via a link or QR code — Always Reviews sends the review link automatically over WhatsApp and email after every completed job, on a 5/10/20-day follow-up schedule if there's no response.
  • Reply to reviews — 4–5★ reviews get a genuine AI-drafted reply (via inbound-gbp-review-capture.json); lower-star reviews route to the business owner instead of an auto-reply, by design (see section 04).
  • Value the mix, not just the positives — the product has no mechanism to suppress or filter which customers get asked; every completed job in the eligible-customer query gets a request, positive or negative outcome unknown in advance.

One detail worth keeping in outreach conversations: a customer needs a Google Account to leave a review, but it doesn't need to be a Gmail address — worth mentioning if a prospective client worries their customers "don't use Google."

03
Writing replies

Google's own style guide for review replies

This is effectively a brief for the AI reply prompt in inbound-gbp-review-capture.json — short, genuine, not promotional, signed by a name.

Do

  • Keep it short — a couple of sentences beats a paragraph
  • Reference something specific from the actual review
  • Sign with a name or initials, so it reads as a real person
  • Only reply when there's something genuine to say

Don't

  • Offer a deal or promotion — they're already a customer
  • Thank every single reviewer publicly out of habit
  • Write anything that reads as a template with names swapped
  • Let a reply sit unposted long enough to look ignored

Worth knowing before it surprises anyone

Google reviews every reply before it posts publicly — usually within 10 minutes, but Google states it can take up to 30 days. A reply that's rejected has to be edited and resubmitted. This matters for expectation-setting with a client: "the AI replied instantly" isn't quite accurate — Post Reply to GBP submits instantly, but the visible posting is on Google's own review timeline, not Always Reviews'.

04
Negative reviews

The gap between Google's advice and what most tools do

This is the section that validates a decision already made in this project — see the messaging framework's "never phrase the low-star handoff as an absence" rule. Google's own guidance backs it up directly.

Reframe

Google explicitly recommends a real reply, from a real person, offering to resolve things offline — not silence, and not a generic auto-reply. Always Reviews' current design (route low-star reviews to the owner instead of auto-replying) is the more Google-aligned choice, not the more cautious one.

Google's five steps for a negative-review reply

Step 1

Check your own records first

Look up what actually happened with that customer before writing anything — don't reply blind.

Step 2

Be honest about what's yours to own

Admit real mistakes plainly. Explain what was outside your control — Google's own example is a weather-related cancellation — without sounding like an excuse.

Step 3

Apologise with actual empathy

Show you understand how the situation felt from their side, not just a scripted "we're sorry for the inconvenience."

Step 4

Sign it as a person

A name or initials — this is what makes a reply feel authentic rather than corporate.

Step 5

Move it offline

Invite them to continue by phone or email. Never share their private info or attack them personally, in the reply or anywhere else.

Why this stays a human step, not an automated one

Steps 1 and 2 need real business context an AI reply generator doesn't have access to — what actually happened, and what was genuinely outside the business's control. That's the underlying reason 1–3★ reviews are flagged for the owner rather than auto-replied to; it's not caution for its own sake, it's that a good reply here structurally requires a human who was there.

05
Mechanics worth knowing

Details that explain "why is this taking so long" questions

Small facts about how Google's review system actually works — useful for setting expectations with a client, or for debugging when something looks broken but isn't.

  • A reply can take up to 30 days to appear publicly, even though it's usually much faster — don't treat a same-day "missing" reply as a bug before checking this.
  • The reviewer is notified when a business replies, and can edit their review afterward — a genuine, specific reply has a real chance to turn a review around, which is part of why a templated one-liner undersells the opportunity.
  • A business must be verified on Google before it can reply to reviews at all — worth checking early in onboarding a new client, before assuming the reply workflow will work end-to-end.
  • Review availability varies by country/region and business category, and can change over time — not every prospective client is guaranteed to have reviews enabled the same way Print Express does.
  • Reviews for service businesses can include the specific service selected by the customer, shown alongside their written review — a detail that could eventually inform which service_type values are worth capturing more precisely at intake.
06
If a review is fake or abusive

Flagging, not fighting, is the sanctioned path

A business can't remove a review it disagrees with — replying with facts is the only direct lever. But a review that actually violates Google's content policies (harassment, spam, conflict of interest, off-topic content) can be reported for Google to review.

Worth adding to any future client-facing FAQ or onboarding material: this is a real, sanctioned escape hatch for the rare genuinely abusive or fake review — distinct from "I don't like what this says about my business," which isn't grounds for removal.
